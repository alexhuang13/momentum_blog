Momentum in
large-batch training

Polyak enlarges the critical batch size,

We study when and how momentum improves large-batch training in the one-pass regime, using power-law kernel regression as a tractable setting. We characterize risk stability, derive scaling laws for the full risk dynamics, and optimize the final-step risk under a fixed data budget.

01

Introduction

Momentum has become a central mechanism in optimizer design for modern large-scale training, underlying popular optimizers such as Adam and Muon. Classical theory views momentum primarily as an acceleration mechanism, quantified through reduced iteration complexity. Yet iteration complexity alone does not fully capture the role of momentum in modern large-scale training, where the optimization regime itself changes as training resources, such as data and hardware memory, scale up.

Modern large-scale training, particularly language-model pretraining, often operates in a one-pass training regime. In this regime, the batch size BBB controls not only hardware parallelism but also the optimization horizon: under a fixed data budget DDD, the number of sequential updates is

T=DB.T=\frac{D}{B}.T=BD​.

Larger batches improve hardware utilization and training throughput, but necessarily reduce the number of optimization steps available per data pass. This creates a fundamental tradeoff between hardware efficiency and data efficiency. We therefore ask:

Can momentum enable larger batches without sacrificing data efficiency, or even improve data efficiency itself?

We address this question for two canonical momentum methods, Polyak and Nesterov, using power-law kernel (PLK) regression as a tractable learning problem. Its infinite-dimensional power-law spectrum spans a broad range of learning timescales, making the effects of momentum across spectral directions analytically accessible. The problem is characterized by two exponents: the capacity exponent β>1\beta>1β>1, which controls the decay of the feature spectrum, and the source exponent s>0s>0s>0, which controls how the target-function energy is distributed across the spectrum.

Our analysis first identifies the admissible hyperparameter region through stability analysis, then derives scaling laws for the risk dynamics, and finally optimizes the final-step risk subject to both the stability constraint and the one-pass constraint T=D/BT=D/BT=D/B.

Why momentum helps large-batch training

The batch-size dependence of momentum can be understood through the per-sample effective learning rate,

Polyak: the admissible effective learning rate

Ignoring the early transient and using T=D/BT=D/BT=D/B, SGD and Polyak obey the same data-level signal–noise tradeoff,

Polyak therefore extends the SGD signal–noise tradeoff to substantially larger batch sizes without changing its optimum, explaining both the enlarged critical batch size and the unchanged best data-scaling exponent.

Nesterov: suppression of noise accumulation

The difference between Nesterov and Polyak stems from the curvature-adaptive damping induced by Nesterov’s look-ahead mechanism, which damps sharper directions more strongly. While leaving the asymptotic power-law signal term unchanged at (kηρ)−s(k\eta_\rho)^{-s}(kηρ​)−s, this mechanism suppresses stochastic noise accumulation. This improves the signal–noise tradeoff itself and thereby achieves better data efficiency in the large-batch regime. At ultra-large batch sizes, however, the limited number of sequential updates T=D/BT=D/BT=D/B becomes the dominant bottleneck, and the two momentum methods recover the same signal-limited scaling.

At small batch sizes, SGD, Polyak, and Nesterov achieve nearly identical risk. 07

Conclusion and discussion

We developed a unified analysis of Polyak and Nesterov momentum in large-batch, one-pass training, characterizing their risk stability, risk dynamics, and optimal data scaling. A central conclusion is that the benefit of momentum depends critically on the batch size. At small batch sizes, neither method improves the data-scaling exponent of vanilla SGD. As the batch size grows, Polyak preserves the small-batch optimal data-scaling exponent over a substantially wider range of batch sizes than SGD, whereas Nesterov further improves the data-scaling exponent in the large-batch regime. At ultra-large batch sizes, training becomes limited by the number of sequential updates, and the two momentum methods again exhibit the same signal-limited scaling.

Our results suggest that learning rate, batch size, and momentum should be designed jointly rather than tuned in isolation. The stability conditions determine how aggressively momentum can be increased at a given learning rate and batch size, while the scaling laws quantify the resulting tradeoff between faster signal learning and stronger noise accumulation. Extending this framework to time-varying learning rates, batch sizes, and momentum factors could therefore provide principled guidance for jointly scaling and scheduling these hyperparameters in large-scale training.
